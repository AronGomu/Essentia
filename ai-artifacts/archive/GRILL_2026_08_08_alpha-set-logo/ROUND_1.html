Form & intent — the serif Alpha mark

Brief corrected: serif, old school, classic — not cursive. Every question below is rebuilt around a roman capital, and every diagram is redrawn. The facts underneath did not change: LOTA-0001-Alpha_0.1 holds exactly 50 cards, set_info.symbol is empty, the package is open so writing trips no immutability gate, and the symbol is a set-level field — wiring all 50 cards is one line, not fifty edits.

Two constraints, read from MSE's own source

  • The mark is rendered into a 60 × 60 pixel box. Not "small" — sixty. This project's card style is 750 × 1046 at 300 dpi, and the rarity box is left 620, top 588, 60 wide, 60 tall. Every stroke you keep has to survive inside that.
  • MSE destroys all texture, on import, unconditionally. The symbol field does not hold a PNG — it holds a vector .mse-symbol. Any image imported is converted to greyscale, hard-thresholded to pure black and white, edge-traced, and discarded. No ink bleed, no plate wear, no distress, no grey, no soft edge, no antialiasing. The mark is a 1-bit silhouette or it is nothing.

That second fact matters more now than it did for a cursive mark. "Old school print press" is a texture-heavy idea — the impression of metal in paper, ink squeeze at the stroke edges, plate wear. None of it can survive. Period feel has to be carried entirely by letterform: serif genre, stroke contrast, apex, weight. That is precisely what Q2 through Q6 decide.

1. What are we actually producing?

You said "logo". MSE's per-set attribute is set_info.symbol — a mark stamped on every card and rendered into a 60 × 60 px box as a 1-bit silhouette. A "logo" in the packaging sense (booster front, website hero, print PDF cover) is a different artifact under different rules: it can carry the words Legend of the Alpha, real texture, and colour. Which one we design governs everything below.

2. Which serif genre?

See the second diagram. "Old school, classic" spans four centuries and four incompatible presses — the book page, the engraved title page, the jobbing print shop, and the very first press. This choice sets stress axis, serif construction, and how much contrast is even period-correct. Everything after it inherits from here.

3. Which glyph is the mark?

"A" still leaves real choices. The capital is monumental and symmetrical; the lowercase double-storey a is a far more distinctive shape and the one where a serif face shows its personality; the monogram encodes the whole set name and the LOTA code.

4. How is the apex treated?

See the third diagram. On a capital A the apex is the signature — it is where a designer declares which century they are in, and it is also the first casualty of a 60 px raster, because a true point lands between pixels and rounds itself off. If you pick the lowercase a in Q3, read this as "how is the terminal treated" instead.

5. What weight and stroke contrast?

See the fourth diagram, and remember the rarity fills: every non-common variation strokes a black border around every contour. A stroke thinner than twice that border does not become a thin stroke — it becomes solid black. Weight and contrast therefore have to be chosen against the render, not against the page.

6. What ornament?

See the fifth diagram. Ornament is where "old school press" is most tempting and most expensive: the box is fixed at 60 px, so anything added beside the letter forces the letter itself to shrink. The inline is the exception — it keeps the width, and pays in a different currency.

7. Freestanding, or contained?

See the sixth diagram. Real set symbols are almost always bare silhouettes, because a container eats the interior space. A container in exchange gives a consistent, instantly recognisable outline across a whole family of sets — and a serif A, being a wide triangle, loses more to a round container than most letters would.

8. One-off, or the first of a family?

The repo names sets by a stable setId (LOTA-0001), which implies a second and third set eventually. If those need marks that look related, the constraint has to be designed in now — a serif family is defined by shared construction rules, and retrofitting them onto a finished one-off never works cleanly.

9. How should the artwork actually be produced?

Your stated process is: I write a prompt, you generate. That works — MSE does accept a raster import. But the source tells us what it does with it: greyscale, hard threshold, edge-trace to vector. A generated image's soft edges do not stay soft, they become permanent wobbly vector contours, and its near-blacks either snap solid or vanish depending on which side of the threshold they land. This bites harder for a serif than it would have for a script: serifs are small rectangles with right angles, and generators are notoriously bad at keeping them consistent across the four corners of one letter. This is the one question where I am arguing with your brief, and you get to overrule me.
